# 8. The Data & AI roadmap

**Decision:** The Data & AI subject has 14 phases and 260 topics, written for a computer science student in the later years of a degree or an engineer early in a career. It runs in one line, from data to models: working with data (statistics, experiments, data platforms), then machine learning, deep learning and language models, then how to run them in practice and how to do it responsibly, then classical AI (search, logic, planning, probabilistic reasoning), specialized problems and the landmark papers.

**Why:** The reader has programmed, and has had discrete math, probability and linear algebra, but has little practice with data or models. The subject has to start from that reader and end where a first job begins: choosing a model, trusting the data, shipping it and being accountable for it.

**How it was checked:** we read the lecture and chapter order of the main university courses (Stanford CS229, CS230, CS231n, CS224N, CS336, CS324, CS329S; Berkeley CS 189; CMU 10-601; MIT 6.390), open textbooks and courses (Dive into Deep Learning, Mathematics for Machine Learning, An Introduction to Statistical Learning, OpenIntro Statistics, Forecasting: Principles and Practice, Fairness and Machine Learning, Interpretable Machine Learning, the Deep Learning book, fast.ai, Google's Machine Learning Crash Course, the Hugging Face LLM course, Karpathy's Zero to Hero), two books on production systems (Fundamentals of Data Engineering and Designing Machine Learning Systems), and primary documents (the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, the EU AI Act summary, the CIDR lakehouse paper, Google's MLOps guide and Rules of Machine Learning, Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems, The ML Test Score, Model Cards, Datasheets for Datasets). The ACM data science and CS2023 reports were checked from search summaries only, because the publisher refused automated downloads. No source's structure was copied. Where they disagree, the order follows our own rule: each topic builds on what came before it.

**What the sources agree on:**

- Every machine learning course opens with supervised learning and the train, validation and test split, then linear models and gradient descent, then generalization (overfitting, bias and variance, regularization), then classic models, and only then neural networks. We follow that, and put evaluation and features beside generalization because they are what a practitioner uses it for.
- Courses taught now no longer stop at neural networks. CS229, CMU 10-601, MIT 6.390 and CS230 each add transformers, language models and responsible AI. Deep learning therefore ends with transformers and generative models, and language models get their own phase.
- Classical AI (search, constraint satisfaction, logic, planning, Bayesian networks and hidden Markov models) is still part of the curriculum documents (the AI knowledge area of CS2023 lists search and knowledge representation) and of CMU 10-601, but no longer the way into the field. It gets its own short phase, after the modern material, because it needs probability and Bayesian inference, and because search and planning lead into reinforcement learning.
- Production courses and books (CS329S, Designing Machine Learning Systems, Google's MLOps guide) agree on the arc: requirements, data, modeling, deployment, monitoring, continual learning, then the human side. That is the Machine Learning in Practice phase.

**Ordering choices worth recording:**

- Data before models. Statistics and data platforms come first, because a model is only as good as its data, and because the experiment and inference ideas (sampling, hypothesis tests, power) are used again when comparing models.
- Math is its own short phase, not scattered through the others. The reader has met it in courses, so each topic is a refresher aimed at what machine learning needs next (the chain rule for backpropagation, cross-entropy for losses, the SVD for PCA).
- Classic models stay, in their own phase. Trees and boosting still win on tabular data, and k-means and PCA are used everywhere. They also teach the vocabulary that deep learning reuses.
- Reinforcement learning is a phase of its own, between deep learning and language models. RLHF and training models to reason are reinforcement learning, so they cannot come before it, and deep reinforcement learning needs neural networks, so it cannot come before deep learning. Its first lesson, bandits, sits earlier still, in the experiments group of Working with Data, because Monte Carlo tree search and adaptive experiments use it.
- Within a phase, a tool comes after what it needs: feature scaling before regularization (both depend on it), embeddings before sequence models, language model prompting after pretraining and adaptation, bandits before Markov decision processes.
- Landmark Papers come last, as System Design's Classic Systems do, ordered by theme and each tied to the chapters it illustrates. They are deep dives that use everything before them.

**Boundaries with the other subjects:**

- **System Design, phase 8 (AI Systems)** covers building and running applications on top of models: serving, caching and routing, semantic search, vector databases, retrieval-augmented generation, context windows and agents, evaluation and guardrails of an application, and monitoring it in production. Data & AI covers how models are made and why they behave as they do. Where both touch a topic (embeddings, evaluation, monitoring, prompt injection), Data & AI teaches the idea and links to the system design topic for the engineering. Retrieval-augmented generation and agents have no topic here; they live in System Design.
- **System Design, phases 3 and 4** already teach warehouses, columnar storage, star schemas, ETL and ELT, batch and stream processing, change data capture, job schedulers, workflow engines and probabilistic structures. The Data Platforms and Pipelines phase links to them and teaches only what they leave out: lakes and lakehouses, table formats, transformations as code, slowly changing dimensions, lineage, data quality, contracts and governance.
- **Algorithms** has dynamic programming and graph algorithms. The Reinforcement Learning group uses value iteration and Q-learning, which build on them, and links back.

**Second pass (an audit of order, gaps and overlap):**

- Order: features moved before generalization; data leakage moved to evaluation; embeddings moved ahead of sequence models; the language model groups reordered (adapting, then using, then reasoning); the margin and probabilistic classifier groups split so generative models come before naive Bayes; bandits moved before MDPs; the landmark language model papers sorted by first release date.
- Gaps filled: classical AI (a new phase of 8), a short history of AI, Jacobians, joint distributions and independence, the multivariate Gaussian, floating point, entity resolution, tests for comparing groups, nonlinear features, linear classifiers, expressiveness of networks, AutoML, harms of generative models, human oversight, copyright and provenance.
- Overlap removed: batch and online prediction, monitoring models and testing models in production (System Design's Model Serving, Monitoring AI Systems and deployment strategies already cover them); the evaluation judges topic (System Design's Evaluation covers model judges and contamination); prompt injection (Guardrails covers it); pipelines as graphs (Workflow Engines, Job Schedulers and Batch Processing); privacy and retention in data governance (Data Privacy and Compliance). One "simple rule beats a model" topic merged into problem framing, and the choice between trees and neural networks moved to Practice, where both are known.
- Intended repetition: a Landmark Papers topic retells the story and experiments of a paper whose idea has its own chapter earlier. The chapter teaches the idea; the paper topic shows how it was shown to work.

**Third pass (checking every source topic against the plan, and every topic's prerequisites against what comes before it):**

- Gaps found by matching each course and textbook topic by topic: tensors and vectorized computation, Hessians and Lagrange multipliers (the SVM needs them), generalized linear models, interpreting regression coefficients, evaluating clusterings, communicating findings, the ingestion and serving stages of the data engineering lifecycle, counting FLOPs and memory (CS336's resource accounting), designing products around wrong predictions and models on devices (Designing Machine Learning Systems), survival analysis (ISLP), implicit feedback in recommenders, and rational agents (the start of the AI knowledge area).
- Order errors found by checking prerequisites: Monte Carlo tree search uses the bandit idea of exploration, which sat in the later Reinforcement Learning group, so bandits moved to the experiments group of Working with Data (adaptive experiments), where they follow A/B tests and Bayesian inference; scaling laws need FLOPs, so compute accounting comes first; decoding belongs with how a language model generates, before pretraining; prompting follows pretraining and precedes fine-tuning, and training models to reason follows chain of thought; requirements come before metrics and baselines.
- Left out on purpose, with the reason: Newton's method and weighted least squares (mentioned inside gradient descent and linear regression), decision theory and double descent (inside generative models and overfitting), video and 3D vision, speech and audio, state space models, mechanistic interpretability, Gaussian processes, and Monte Carlo methods as their own topic (inside Bayesian inference). Each is real, but would be a third-level detail of a topic that exists.
- Checked and kept: the Landmark Papers repetition, the one-topic groups (they hold a single idea that has no neighbor), and the few topics that echo System Design (ingestion, serving data, embeddings), each limited to what System Design does not say.

**Final pass (prerequisites checked by script, titles checked against System Design, Algorithms and each other):**

- About 110 prerequisite pairs were written down (for example RLHF needs policy gradients, SVMs need Lagrange multipliers, naive Bayes needs independence and generative models) and checked against the order. Two violations were found and fixed: language models came before reinforcement learning, which RLHF and reasoning training need, so reinforcement learning became its own phase before them (with a new topic on actor-critic methods and PPO, the algorithm RLHF uses); feature selection came before the L1 regularization it relies on, so it moved after regularization.
- Landmark Papers that were the same document as a chapter were removed: Hidden Technical Debt (the chapter on technical debt is that paper), Model Cards (the chapter is the paper) and the scaling laws paper (the chapter has the same title and covers it, with Chinchilla kept for its correction). The word2vec chapter was renamed so it does not repeat the word2vec paper topic.
- Gaps filled: grouping and window functions in SQL (the one analysis skill System Design's SQL topics do not cover), and prediction intervals and uncertainty.
- Title similarity against the other two subjects found no real duplicates. The lifecycle topics were renamed to tell the machine learning project lifecycle from the data engineering lifecycle.
- One structural repetition remains and is accepted: Landmark Papers retell the story and results of papers whose ideas have their own chapter (the same pattern as System Design's Classic Systems). An article in that phase must teach what the chapter does not: the problem as it stood, the experiments and results, and what was later superseded. If this proves too repetitive, the whole phase can be dropped with no effect on the others.

**Costs:** At 260 topics this is the largest subject on the site, and the Landmark Papers, Classical AI and Specialized Problems phases are the easiest to postpone, since no other phase depends on them. Several early topics (the Math phase, parts of Foundations) are refreshers that some readers will skip. Years for the older landmark papers (the Perceptron, backpropagation, LSTM) were not confirmed from a primary page; each is verified when its article is written.

**Alternatives:** Split into two subjects, Data and AI (a cleaner line, but the reader of a first data job needs both, and the middle, ML in practice, would belong to neither); drop Math and send the reader to a textbook (shorter, but the site promises that each topic needs only what came before); fold Landmark Papers into the phases they illustrate (fewer topics, but a phase of papers read in order is how most people meet the field).
